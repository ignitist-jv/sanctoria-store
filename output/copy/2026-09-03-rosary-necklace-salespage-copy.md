> WHAT GETS PASSED DOWN

**H2**

> Somewhere in this house

**Body**

Most Catholic families have one. It turns up in a drawer after a funeral, in a jewelry box, in the pocket of a coat nobody had the heart to give away. Half the beads are worn smooth on one side, because a thumb sat there for fifty years in the same place every night.

Nobody in the family can tell you where it was bought. That was never the part that mattered. What mattered was whose hands were on it, and how many times, and for whom.

The faith does not skip a generation because anyone stopped believing. It skips because nothing physical got handed across, and there was nothing in a young woman's hand to make the asking feel possible.

> A rosary is the one thing in a Catholic house that gets more valuable the more it is used.

**Black matte wooden beads.** Round, smooth, sanded rather than polished. Wood has weight without coldness, and it warms in a hand within a minute of being held. It does not slip when the fingers are shaking. It is the same material a monk would have counted on eight hundred years ago, for the same reason: it is quiet, it is honest, and it lasts.

**Black braided cord, knotted between every bead.** Not a chain. A chain has links, and a link is the thing that opens on the day it is dropped on a stone floor. Cord with a knot on either side of every bead has no link to open. If it is ever cut, the beads stay where the knots hold them. It is also silent. A cord rosary in a coat pocket does not announce itself in a Mass.

**Silver-tone Saint Benedict medals set along its whole length.** Not one medal at the center and plain beads everywhere else. Small round Saint Benedict medals are spaced through the decades, so wherever a thumb happens to stop, it is on the same prayer of protection the Church has used for centuries. **A black wooden crucifix with a silver Christ.** The corpus in silver against dark wood, the INRI plaque above it, and set into the very center of the cross, where the beams meet, a small silver Saint Benedict medal. The crucifix is the part a thumb ends on, and the part a child looks at first.

**The palette is matte black and silver, and that is deliberate.** It does not read as jewelry. It reads as something that belongs to somebody who prays. It can sit on a bedside table for thirty years and never look dated, because it was never in fashion to begin with.

## 6. WHAT THE SAINT BENEDICT MEDAL LETTERS MEAN

**Divider text**

> ★ WHAT THE LETTERS SAY

**H2**

> Those letters are not decoration.
> They are a prayer, one letter per word.

**Body**

The letters ringing a Saint Benedict medal are initials. Each one stands for a whole word of an old Latin prayer, compressed so that the entire prayer fits around the rim of a disc smaller than a coin. The Church has been striking them this way for centuries.

**Around the edge of the medal: C S P B.** *Crux Sancti Patris Benedicti.* The Cross of our Holy Father Benedict.

**Down the upright of the cross on the medal: C S S M L.** *Crux Sacra Sit Mihi Lux.* May the holy cross be my light.

**Across the arms: N D S M D.** The second half of that same line, asking that nothing else be given the lead.

**At the top, the single word: PAX.** Peace. It is the Benedictine motto, and it has been for fifteen hundred years.

**Around the outer rim: V R S N S M V S M Q L I V B.** The initials of an older Latin prayer of protection that the Church has prayed for centuries. The full Latin and the full English translation are printed on the card that ships in the box.

That last part matters more than it sounds. A rosary that cannot be explained is a rosary that gets admired and put away. This one arrives with its own translation, so a grandmother asked *what do the letters mean* at a Sunday table can answer the question properly, and a granddaughter gets an answer worth remembering.
