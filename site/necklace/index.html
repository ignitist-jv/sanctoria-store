The Reflex

Watch what the hand does
when the phone rings at 11:40 at night

It happens before the thought does. A screen lights the dark with a daughter's name on it, and the hand is already flat against the center of the chest.

Nobody is taught that. Watch any woman hear something she was not ready for. A waiting room. A kitchen doorway. A parking lot with the car keys still in her hand. Same hand, same place, every time.

It is the oldest prayer there is and it has no words in it. Before Lord, before please, before the name gets out, the body has gone looking for something to hold.

And almost always it finds a blouse. A cardigan. Skin. Nothing at all. Years of praying for these people, and when the fear comes at them sideways, there is nothing under the palm.

The reflex is already there. It is only missing something to land on.

Where Holy Things End Up

Blessed things end up in drawers. That is not a lack of faith.

The confirmation medal is in the little dish on the dresser. A rosary in the car console. A holy card behind a photograph in the hallway. Most Catholic homes are full of blessed things, and almost none of them are within reach at the moment they are wanted. That is not faith failing. That is a drawer.

A bracelet is better, because at least it comes along. But a wrist is not where a frightened woman puts her hand. Nobody has ever heard bad news and grabbed her own wrist.

The chest is the place. Center of the sternum, right where a collar closes. It is where the hand goes at the bad news and the good news both, and it is the one spot on the body that nothing blessed is ever hanging.

So this was made to hang there and stay there. Flat against the skin under a blouse. Into the shower, the dishwater, the rain, the hospital corridor, and back out again. Nothing in an ordinary day is a reason to take it off, which is exactly why it never starts living in a drawer.

The Whole Point

The words do not always come.
The hand already knows where to go.

This is the devotion nobody has to remember, because the body reaches for it first. On at six in the morning. Still on at midnight. Nothing about the day asks for it, and then one afternoon the news arrives and the palm closes over a cross and a blessed medal instead of empty cotton.

Why Two

One says whose you are. One asks Him to guard them.

For more than fifteen centuries, families have turned to Saint Benedict when they wanted their people covered. His medal has been set into the foundations of houses, hung over cribs, tucked above a car visor, carried in a soldier's pocket. It is what the Church has always reached for when a mother says protect them.

But a medal on its own is a request with no name on it. So this one hangs beside a plain Latin cross, both on the same ring, both under the same palm.

The Names

Most people who wear one have been carrying a name for years

Ask around a parish rosary group. Every woman in the room has one. Usually more than one, and usually the same ones she had ten years ago.

One stopped going to Mass and will not talk about it. One is in a marriage that has gone quiet in a way her mother recognizes. One is waiting on results and keeps saying he feels fine. One is still small enough to climb into a lap, and nobody can follow her everywhere.

The names get said at the kitchen table in the morning. By ten o'clock it is the electric bill. Everyone means to come back to it. Everyone means to again tomorrow.

Nobody needs to be told to try harder. These women have carried these people for years, and tomorrow will be as full as today was. The distance does not close because somebody pushed.

Their own mothers wore something every day. A medal, a scapular, something small under the collar. It was never a decision. It was just there.

Then one afternoon at a red light a hand lands at the center of a chest, thumb on the medal, and the names have been said again without anyone deciding to.

Something that keeps asking after the day takes over.
