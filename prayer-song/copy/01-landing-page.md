=== NOTES ===

CONTROL HEADLINE
"Their name, sung in a prayer that keeps being prayed."
Why this one: it carries the product (a song with a name in it), the mechanism (keeps being prayed) and the positioning (prayer, not gift) in one line, and it asserts nothing about the reader. The eyebrow supplies the antecedent for "their".

HEADLINE BANK (10 alternatives)
1. A spoken prayer is said once. A sung one keeps being prayed.
   Idea: pure mechanism, contrast lead. Strongest idea, weakest product clarity, so it needs the eyebrow and sub to say what is sold.
2. A prayer for someone you love, sung by name.
   Idea: plain product clarity. The safest read for cold traffic. No mechanism.
3. Have their name set into a prayer that can be played again and again.
   Idea: commissioning verb ("have it set"), makes it feel made to order.
4. The prayer said at the kitchen table, set to music so it stays.
   Idea: concrete domestic image from the customer's own world, the AQuietDevotion register that is winning in the ad account.
5. One name. One prayer. Sung, so it can be prayed every day.
   Idea: rhythm and brevity, good for a narrow mobile column.
6. The Church has always sung what it most wanted to keep.
   Idea: tradition as authority. Curiosity lead, product revealed in the sub.
7. A prayer song written for one person, with their name in every chorus.
   Idea: feature led and literal. Useful as a control against the mechanism headlines.
8. Some names have been prayed for years. This is a way to keep praying them.
   Idea: speaks to the long carried name (the VOC pain of praying for the same person for ten years or more) in third person.
9. What if the prayer for them could be played in the car, at the bedside, and at the kitchen sink?
   Idea: question lead, future pacing through places.
10. Not a gift song. A prayer, sung over one person by name.
   Idea: explicit differentiation from the sentimental gift category. Uses a negative, which often outperforms.

COMPLIANCE NOTES AND THINGS I WAS UNSURE OF
1. Second person. The page never asserts the reader's faith, grief, family trouble or health. The words "you" and "your" do not appear anywhere in the page body. The reader is addressed through "tell us" imperatives and through third person ("many Catholic women", "the one who had it made", "she"). The FAQ uses "she" and "her" for the buyer, matching the necklace page. 2. Saint Augustine. "The one who sings prays twice" is hedged as "an old saying, often credited to Saint Augustine", because the exact sentence is not found in his writings (the Catechism cites it at 1156 with a reference to his commentary on the Psalms). The hedge is deliberate. Do not tighten it to a direct quote.
3. No Scripture is quoted on the page, so nothing needs checking against the Douay-Rheims. If an epigraph is wanted, Psalm 95:1 in the Douay-Rheims numbering fits ("Sing ye to the Lord a new canticle: sing to the Lord, all the earth.").
